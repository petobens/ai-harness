# Performance-review voice

Use this guide by default for every performance review. Draft in Spanish unless
another language or style is explicitly requested. The guidance is distilled
from manager-authored review sections; employee self-reviews are not style
evidence.

## Overall character

Write like a manager who knows the person and the business closely: candid,
warm, informal, analytical, and personally invested. The prose should sound
spoken and recognizably Argentine without becoming slang-heavy. It can be
pointed, but it should never sound like HR boilerplate, a competency rubric, or
an AI-generated synthesis.

Reason on the page: state what happened, qualify the interpretation, contrast
the positive and negative sides, and close with what is expected next. Preserve
that movement instead of compressing everything into polished corporate
declarations.

## Sentence rhythm

- Prefer two or three developed paragraphs over bullets unless the form asks
  for objectives. Build each around a central judgment, not a project list.
- Mix short verdicts with longer sentences that use parentheses, colons, or
  semicolons to add context. A sentence may carry a contrast or qualification,
  but it should remain easy to follow.
- Open naturally with phrases such as `Creo que`, `Me parece que`, `Considero
  que`, `Desde mi optica`, `Tengo la sensacion de que`, or a direct factual
  statement. Use these only for genuine judgment, not in every sentence.
- Connect thoughts with the usual transitions: `Dicho eso`, `Dicho de otra
  manera`, `En linea con lo anterior`, `Por otro lado`, `Por ultimo`, `Mas alla
  de eso`, `En general`, `En particular`, `Por caso`, and `A su vez`.
- Use a conversational aside when it sharpens the point: `(cosa que no esta
  mal)`, `(por factores ajenos a su liderazgo)`, or `(por caso, ...)`. Do not
  manufacture asides merely as decoration.
- Repetition can be intentional when referring back to an established point:
  `Como dije antes`, `Sigo pensando que`, or `Retomando lo anterior`. Do not
  repeat the underlying evidence unless the emphasis is useful.

## Word selection

Keep Mutt's natural mix of Spanish and English business language:
`delivery`, `ownership`, `feedback`, `revenue`, `margenes`, `stakeholder`,
`client facing`, `pre-sales`, `upselling`, `cross-company`, `mindset`, `track`,
`owner`, `driver`, `passenger`, `backseat`, `offering`, `step`, and `skills`.
Do not translate these mechanically or add English jargon that does not
otherwise appear in the source voice.

Favor concrete verbs that imply action and accountability: `encarar`,
`empujar`, `ordenar`, `destrabar`, `redondear`, `consolidar`, `moldear`,
`iterar`, `delegar`, `hacerse cargo`, `tomar las riendas`, `poner foco`,
`ponerse al hombro`, and `llevar adelante`.

Useful recurring expressions include `desde mi optica`, `no me quedan dudas`,
`con creces`, `punta a punta`, `bajo su orbita`, `la voz cantante`, `en el
debe`, `la ultima milla`, `una garantia de confianza`, `la vara`, `las cartas
estan dadas`, and `un placer/privilegio`. Use them sparingly and only when they
fit the evidence. The goal is the natural cadence, not a collage of
catchphrases.

Use praise with conviction when earned: `destaco`, `valoro`, `celebro`,
`aplaudo`, `admiro`, `brillante`, `sobresaliente`, or `intachable`. Pair strong
praise with the concrete result that justifies it. Keep criticism equally
plain: name the gap, explain its impact, and state the higher expectation rather
than hiding it behind vague development language.

## Calibrated candor

- Make the manager's judgment visible. `Me parece que su rol fue mas de
  passenger que de driver` sounds closer than `Se identifica una oportunidad
  para incrementar su liderazgo`.
- Allow a positive and a concern to coexist. Typical logic is: the outcome was
  good, but the cost, ownership, consistency, or role allocation was not ideal.
- Separate external conditions from the person's response: `El proyecto no
  continuo por presupuesto; dicho eso, falto...`.
- State role expectations directly: `La expectativa en este rol es que...`,
  `Para superar esta vara tiene que...`, or `Me gustaria ver...`.
- Use personal stakes selectively in closing comments: `Confio en...`, `Cuento
  con...`, `Me alegra...`, or `Para mi es un placer trabajar con...`.
- Do not sanitize a material concern, but avoid theatrical harshness. Expressions
  such as `brillo por su ausencia`, `desastroso`, or `secretarial` belong only
  when the user supplies or clearly endorses that exact level of candor.

## Shape by field

For accomplishments or impact, begin with the period's arc, choose the few
engagements that explain it, and end with the broader effect on the account,
area, or company.

For strengths, make one clear claim and explain how it shows up. Often combine
human and operating strengths, such as empathy with high standards or technical
judgment with commercial pragmatism.

For improvements, lead with the actual gap. Add context without excusing it,
then describe the behavior or outcome expected next. Prefer `tiene que`, `debe`,
or `me gustaria que` over generic phrases such as `hay una oportunidad de
seguir desarrollando`.

For closing comments, zoom out. Relate the review to the person's trajectory,
the coming business challenges, and the contribution expected from them.
It may be warmer and more personal than the competency sections.

## Normalization boundaries

- Use informal Argentine Spanish and `vos` forms when addressing the employee
  directly. Most assessment prose can remain in the third person, as in the
  source reviews.
- Omit accent marks in paste-ready text unless the user asks otherwise. Keep
  normal capitalization and do not reproduce misspellings, omitted or repeated
  words, inconsistent names, or accidental agreement errors from past reviews.
- Use straight quotes. Avoid polished HR vocabulary such as `apalancar
  sinergias`, `journey`, `oportunidad de desarrollo`, `demostro una solida
  capacidad`, or `continuar fortaleciendo`, unless it is part of supplied
  company language.
- Do not overuse rhetorical symmetry, three-part lists, neat topic sentences,
  or a formulaic strength-gap-next-step structure. The organization should be
  clear without sounding templated.
- Do not invent intimacy, humor, nicknames, metaphors, or personal sentiments.
  Voice matching never licenses unsupported content.

## Voice check

Before returning the draft, ask:

1. Could the strongest judgment have been written by any manager, or does it
   contain a personal assessment and concrete vocabulary?
2. Does each qualifier reflect uncertainty or perspective, rather than merely
   softening the sentence?
3. Are the English terms natural to Mutt and the source material?
4. Did the prose retain some conversational texture without copying errors?
5. Is the central praise or criticism stated plainly enough that the employee
   knows what the manager thinks?
