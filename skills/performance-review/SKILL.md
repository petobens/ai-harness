# Performance Review

Write a candid, concise manager review grounded in the requested review period.
Use the employee's self review as context, not as the outline: the manager
feedback should add judgment instead of repeating the employee's claims.

## Inputs

Read the full relevant scope before drafting:

- the self review and whether it is submitted or still a draft;
- 1:1 notes from the review period;
- the employee's role and seniority;
- the manager's preliminary rating and observations; and
- the form fields and who can see each one.

Treat 1:1 notes as evidence, not as a verified transcript. Distinguish reported
outcomes, employee claims, manager observations, and the model's inferences. Use
qualified language when the evidence does not support a definitive statement.
Do not treat an omission from a draft or visibly truncated self review as
evidence of weak performance.

Do not let events after the review period change the rating. They may inform a
private risk or forward-looking note only when useful, and should be identified
as later context rather than evidence for the period.

Ask a clarifying question only when a missing choice would materially change the
assessment. Otherwise infer the intended form and draft a recommendation.

## Review cadence

Determine whether the form is a mid-year check or the full-year review. At Mutt,
the mid-year review is a lightweight checkpoint within an annual performance
cycle: its score is indicative, not the final annual verdict. Keep it brief and
focus on progress during the first half, the clearest strengths or gaps, and one
or two priorities for the second half. Do not force exhaustive coverage of every
competency or treat six months of evidence as a completed annual trajectory.

Use the full-year review for a more comprehensive assessment of sustained
performance, development across the year, and readiness for the next step.
When a mid-year form includes a promotion field, assess current readiness only;
do not turn it into a promise or forecast of an annual promotion decision.

## Mutt competency matrix

For Mutt reviews, inspect the current
[competency matrix](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1D7z9RsdSSGlV75Rs5Q15bu1egE8EkWhJcKX3SjWA49g/edit?usp=sharing)
before assessing the employee. Inspect the workbook structure first and use the
latest sheet contents rather than copying its wording into this skill.

Identify the employee's exact track and step from the assessment context, such
as `IC4`, `M3`, or `S1`. Find the rubric tab by its competency and step headers;
it is currently `V1`. Read the competency-name column and the column whose header
matches the step. Compare the evidence against its description, scope, impact
and delivery, communication, knowledge, and Muttiness expectations. If the step
is missing and different steps would materially change the verdict, ask the user
instead of inferring it from a job title.

Use the matrix as a rubric, not as a checklist to reproduce in the review. For a
mid-year check, mention only the competencies that materially explain the rating
or next-period focus. Do not let one isolated competency mechanically determine
the score; weigh its importance to the role, the breadth of evidence, and the
overall pattern. If the matrix cannot be accessed, say so briefly and proceed
from the supplied role context rather than inventing its criteria.

## Assessment

Assess performance against the expectations of the role, not effort, activity,
or the employee's self-rating alone. Distinguish among:

- outcomes the employee produced;
- external conditions they could not control;
- how well they anticipated, responded to, or mitigated those conditions; and
- behaviors expected at their level, such as ownership, judgment, commercial
  impact, delegation, stakeholder management, or developing leaders.

Recommend the rating supported by the evidence, even when the user begins with a
tentative view. Use the available scale exactly. For the common three-point
scale:

- `Partially meets expectations`: material role expectations were not met, even
  if there were useful contributions or legitimate external constraints.
- `Meets expectations`: solid impact at the expected level, with meaningful but
  non-disqualifying areas to improve.
- `Exceeds expectations`: sustained impact materially beyond the role, supported
  by specific evidence rather than workload or breadth alone.

Treat promotion readiness separately from performance. A strong or exceeds
rating does not imply readiness for the next level; consider sustained evidence,
scope, delegation, and time in role.

## Writing the feedback

Lead with an overall judgment, then support it with a small number of concrete
examples. Prefer precise statements such as "renewed a key account and put a
weekly operating review in place" over vague praise such as "helped a lot."

Balance strengths and improvements without using a formulaic compliment sandwich.
State the central gap plainly and connect it to the role. When the employee did
valuable coordination but limited individual execution, distinguish ideation,
review, presentation, team coordination, and end-to-end delivery instead of
crediting all output to one person. Apply the same care in reverse: do not assign
an entire team failure to one person without evidence of their responsibility.

For commercial roles, do not equate commercial ability only with traditional
selling. Building stakeholder trust, finding needs, shaping proposals, protecting
renewals, and expanding accounts are also commercial work.

When workload, part-time availability, missing middle management, or a ramp-up
period materially affected delivery, include that context without using it to
erase the underlying expectation. Treat health and other sensitive personal
information more carefully: include it only when the user wants it considered,
it is appropriate for the field's audience, and without inferring a diagnosis
or unsupported causal link.

Make improvement feedback actionable with one or two examples and a clear
next-period expectation. A useful test is whether the reader can tell what
should change in day-to-day behavior.

## Public and private fields

Keep employee-visible feedback constructive, direct, and fair. Include sensitive
judgments only when the employee needs them to improve and phrase them in terms
of observable behavior and role expectations rather than personality labels.

Use the private calibration comment for candid context such as:

- confidence in the person's ability to resolve problems independently;
- whether their departure creates company risk or mainly redistributes workload;
- motivation, role-fit, or key-person concerns;
- why external factors do or do not change the rating;
- what evidence is still needed to evaluate individual output or next-level
  readiness; and
- material attitude or collaboration concerns that need calibration context.

Do not merely summarize the visible feedback in the private field. Add new
calibration information, and do not leak private calibration context into the
employee-visible field.

## Output

Return only the fields the user requests or the form requires. When the complete
form is requested, use:

1. `Feedback para <nombre>`: two or three short paragraphs for mid-year, ready
   to paste; expand only when a full-year review or the evidence warrants it.
2. `Nota`: the exact rating label.
3. `Promocion`: the exact readiness label.
4. `Comentario privado`: one concise paragraph for calibration.

Keep field labels outside the paste-ready prose so the feedback itself does not
gain unnecessary headings.

Avoid repeating a point across paragraphs or fields. Repeat it once only when the
emphasis is genuinely useful. Do not list every project: choose the examples that
best explain the rating.

Match the manager's natural language and degree of informality. For Pedro's
Spanish reviews, default to informal Argentine Spanish, use qualifiers such as
`creo`, `me parece`, and `sospecho` where they reflect genuine judgment, and omit
accent marks when producing paste-ready text unless he asks otherwise. Keep
English role and business terms that he naturally uses, such as `ownership`,
`client facing`, `heavy lifting`, `portfolio`, and `revenue`. Match intentional
style choices, not accidental typos or unclear grammar.

Before returning the draft, check that every material claim is traceable to the
provided evidence, the rating follows from the written feedback, promotion is
assessed independently, the private comment adds rather than repeats context,
and no example or concern appears more often than its importance warrants.
